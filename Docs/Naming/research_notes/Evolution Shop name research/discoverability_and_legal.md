# The practical side of naming an indie game: discoverability, uniqueness, trademarks, international use, and renaming

Research notes, gathered 2026-11-03, for screening candidate names for the first-person PC indie game now called "Evolution Shop" (growing coloured cultures, hatching strange aquatic creatures, and running a creature shop and lab; Windows, likely Steam). This is research, not legal advice. Anything about trademarks below is a screening method; a registrable, safe-to-use name needs a trademark attorney's clearance opinion in each market that matters.

Source quality note: Valve documents renaming, but not Steam's search ranking or what happens to wishlists on a rename. Most evidence on search behaviour comes from game-press coverage of Valve's search updates and from developers' own devlogs. Several web pages on Rocket League's old name were low-quality content farms; only the Wikipedia article's quotes are used here.

## 1. Store and web search: how titles are found, why common words get buried, and whether invented words help

### Takeaway
Steam's search began as a match on game names and, since 2023, also suggests tags, developers, publishers and franchises whose names match the query. So a title made of common words, or of words that are also tag names, competes with every other game and every tag sharing those words. On Google and YouTube, a generic title like "99999", "Hollow" or "Evolution Shop" is drowned out by unrelated results. Indie developers and naming guides advise a distinctive or invented word, ideally paired with something that hints at the game, and testing the name in real searches before committing.

### Cited Findings
- Steam's store name is the one used "within the Steam store, including search results". It is set separately from the Steamworks name used in the library and community, and from each package's name — [Steamworks Documentation: Changing your game's name](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/editing/name)
- In May 2023 Valve upgraded the Steam store search bar. Before, it matched only game names. Now its suggestions also show tags, developers, publishers and franchises when they look like a good match: typing "dungeon" shows tags such as "Dungeon Crawler" beside games with "Dungeon" in the title. Coverage also reported the update handles misspellings and typos much better — [GamingOnLinux, May 2023](https://gamingonlinux.com/2023/05/valve-gave-steam-store-search-a-very-useful-upgrade); [Game Developer: Steam games can now be searched for by developer, franchise and tag](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/steam-games-can-now-be-searched-for-by-developer-franchise-and-tag)
- Valve's own explanation for its Steam Labs "Query Expansion" experiment said the existing search "treats all tags as unrelated pieces of information". A game tagged RTS but not Real-Time and Strategy would not be found by a search for Real-Time + Strategy. The experiment was opt-in; I found no confirmation it became the default — [PCGamesN: Query Expansion](https://www.pcgamesn.com/steam/query-expansion); [Engadget: smarter tag searches](https://www.engadget.com/steam-smarter-tags-expirement-180058068.html)
- Real case, generic name buried on YouTube and Google: the developer of "99999" called it "a bad idea for a name". Searching it on YouTube returned videos of other games, including Plants vs Zombies, and "99999 game" didn't help. The developer wrote that indies lack the marketing power to "make a name ours", so a generic name risks being "drown[ed] out by similar words/term from other search results". "Googling Gunkid on the other hand, gives you near zero hits on anything videogame-related", so the game became "Gunkid 99" to be "more easily searchable" (devlog from about late 2018) — [Gunkid 99 devlog, itch.io](https://yongjustyong.itch.io/gunkid99/devlog/48419/gunkid-99-previously-99999-name-change-and-updates)
- Real case: the horror game "Hollow" was renamed "Aberrenti" because "Hollow was a temp name and too generic" (28 June 2024) — [Aberrenti devlog, itch.io](https://itch.io/devlog/756437/renamed-the-game.amp)
- Game Developer naming guide (Yannick Elahee, 2018): name for "Google Searches. You want to appear first", and "pick a name that doesn't have competitors on Google". Its example: the game Super Massive was renamed Steredenn after a big publisher was already using the first name. It says to avoid overused words (Age, War, Battle, Craft, Heroes, Dungeon). If you use a generic word, pair it with an original word (Drifting Lands), or use a longer structure ("Dark Age of Camelot", "Double Kick Heroes", "Mother Russia Bleeds"). It also warns against names players confuse with others (Battlerite, Battlegrounds and Battleborn; Warface and Warframe) — [Game Developer: How to name a game](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-to-name-a-game)
- General naming guides found in search warn that a generic name hurts SEO and makes the game hard for players to find, and that it should be easy to spell from hearing it. One cites "Light Of Lamps", whose acronym collides with League of Legends (LoL), as a reason to check acronyms — search summary of [FCHQ: Tips on how to create a name for your game](https://fchq.io/news/tips-to-creating-a-game-name) and [Buildbox: 7 steps to finding the perfect name](https://www.buildbox.com/how-to-name-a-game-in-7-steps) (pages not opened individually; attribution between the two is uncertain)
- Players who can't say a title hesitate to talk about it, and a Japanese case study on Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 describes reading, typing and UI search problems with a foreign title. Word of mouth and search depend on people being able to type what they heard — search summary of [Game Developer: Should you localize your game's title?](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/should-you-localize-your-game-s-title-) and related localisation threads (see section 4)

### Inferences
- **"Evolution Shop" under this check:** both words are common English words. "Evolution" appears in many game titles and in non-game content (biology, Pokémon evolutions, the "Evolution" board game). "Shop" is a generic genre word, close to Steam's shop and management vocabulary. Since the 2023 update, typing either word in Steam's search bar will also bring up matching tags, franchises and many other titles, so the game is unlikely to be the top suggestion unless the full phrase is typed. On Google and YouTube, "evolution shop" will compete with biology content, game "evolution" mechanics and real shops. By the Gunkid test, it fails.
- Invented words help findability (Gunkid, Steredenn, Valheim), but cost meaning and pronounceability (see sections 4 and 6). The common advice is a distinctive coined or rare word plus a familiar word that signals the game.
- **Procedure: search-collision check** (run for each candidate; record the results):
  1. Steam: type the exact name in the store search bar. Note what autocomplete suggests: games, tags, franchises. Then open `https://store.steampowered.com/search/?term=<name>` and count games whose title contains the name or its main word. **Conflict:** any game with the same or nearly the same title, a franchise of that name, or a Steam tag with that exact word.
  2. Google: search the bare name, then the name plus "game". **Pass:** after launch the game could plausibly own the first page, meaning today's first page has no other game and no big brand. **Conflict:** another game, app or well-known product on page 1.
  3. YouTube, Twitch and TikTok: search the bare name. **Conflict:** popular videos or streams of another game or product under that name (99999's case), or a hashtag dominated by an unrelated topic.
  4. Say the name aloud to someone who hasn't seen it, and have them type it into a search box. **Conflict:** they can't spell it, or the spelling they type finds something else.

### Gaps
- Valve doesn't publish how Steam's search ranks titles: exact versus partial match, word weight, or whether common words are discounted. I found no official documentation, only press coverage of the 2023 update.
- I didn't find Chris Zukowski's (howtomarketagame.com) naming advice in this session. Searches returned only his general Steam-page advice that "clarity is king" ([Unity blog: 5 tips & strategies for marketing indie games](https://unity.com/blog/tips-strategies-for-marketing-indie-games)). His blog should be checked directly for a naming post.
- No quantitative study found on title genericness versus wishlists or sales.
- I didn't run the Steam, Google or YouTube searches for "Evolution Shop" in this session; the inference above is reasoning, not a counted result.

## 2. Uniqueness checks: existing games, stores, social handles, domains and hashtags

### Takeaway
No single tool covers it. Developers search each store and platform directly (Steam, itch.io, the App Store and Google Play, plus SteamDB for unreleased apps), then check domains and handles. Apple's App Store also requires a distinctive name under 30 characters, with no stuffed keywords or others' trademarks. A collision with an existing game is both a search problem and a trademark risk (see section 3).

### Cited Findings
- Naming guides advise a quick web search to make sure the title isn't taken, and checking a registrar for the .com or .gg domain — search summary of [FCHQ](https://fchq.io/news/tips-to-creating-a-game-name) and [Buildbox](https://www.buildbox.com/how-to-name-a-game-in-7-steps) (not opened individually)
- Real collision, Stardew Valley: its first name, "Sprout Valley", was dropped by Eric Barone, who later called it hard or "not pleasant to say" and an awful name. One report says he also worried it sounded like a Harvest Moon location. A different pixel-art farming game called "Sprout Valley" was released on Steam in September 2023 — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/stardew-valley-creator-says-the-games-name-just-came-to-me-after-the-original-was-not-pleasant-to-say); [Nintendo Everything](https://nintendoeverything.com/stardew-valley-was-originally-called-sprout-valley-plus-other-development-changes/); [Cool3C (Chinese)](https://www.cool3c.com/article/209118)
- Real collision with an existing big title: the developer of "Diapers, Please!" got its Steam page approved as "Ministry of Order", renamed to avoid resembling Papers, Please, and asked players to wishlist the renamed page — [VoltekPlay devlog, itch.io](https://voltekplay.itch.io/diapers-please/devlog/901651/big-news-our-steam-page-is-now-live)
- Real collision: "Super Massive" became "Steredenn" because a big publisher already used the first name — [Game Developer: How to name a game](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-to-name-a-game)
- Apple App Store Review Guideline 2.3.7 asks developers to "choose a unique app name" and accurate keywords, and not to pack metadata with trademarked terms, popular app names or irrelevant phrases. App names are limited to 30 characters, down from 50 at WWDC in June 2017 — [Apple Developer Forums thread quoting 2.3.7](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/124046); [Phiture: App Store character limit](https://phiture.com/asostack/app-store-introduces-50-character-limit-of-app-names-138477cbf92b/); [AppTweak: App name guidelines](https://www.apptweak.com/en/aso-blog/app-name-guidelines-and-best-practices?lang=en-US)
- A Steam App ID is permanent. Third-party tools such as SteamDB and Steam App ID finders can look a game up by name or ID, including pages that aren't released yet — [Steam Page Analyzer: App ID finder](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/tools/steam-app-id-finder) (vendor page); [Steam Community thread on finding an app's ID](https://steamcommunity.com/discussions/forum/1/7074686732306817122)

### Inferences
- **Procedure: existing-game check.**
  1. Steam store search, as in section 1. Also search SteamDB by name, which also lists Coming Soon and unreleased apps that the store hides.
  2. itch.io: search the name (itch.io/search?q=<name>). Many jam games and prototypes live only there.
  3. Apple App Store and Google Play: search the name. Mobile has the most name squatting, and App Store names must be unique.
  4. Console stores and other PC stores (GOG, Epic, Xbox, PlayStation, Nintendo eShop).
  5. Wikipedia and MobyGames for older or retail games under the same name.
  - **Conflict:** any game, or any app in the same category, with the same name or a confusingly similar one (same main word plus a similar theme). For example, an existing creature, evolution or pet game using "Evolution".
- **Procedure: handle and domain check.** Try the exact name and short variants (with "game" or "hq" added) as handles on X, TikTok, YouTube (@handle), Instagram, Bluesky, Reddit (a subreddit) and Discord vanity invites. Check .com, .gg and .io at a registrar. Search each candidate hashtag on TikTok and X to see what it's already used for.
  - **Conflict:** the exact handle taken by an active account in games or a related field. Taken by a dormant account is a minor problem. A hashtag dominated by an unrelated big topic is a discoverability conflict.
- **"Evolution Shop" under this check:** "evolution" handles, domains and hashtags are almost certainly taken by unrelated uses (biology, fitness, brands). Unverified in this session; see Gaps.

### Gaps
- I didn't verify which tools for checking many handles at once (namechk and others) are current and reliable, so none is recommended here.
- I didn't search the stores for "Evolution Shop" or "Evolution" games in this session. From memory, unverified here: North Star Games' board game "Evolution" has a digital adaptation, "Evolution: The Video Game", on Steam. If true, that's a directly relevant prior use of "Evolution" for a game about evolving creatures, and the screening step should confirm it and check its trademarks.

## 3. Trademarks: the risks, the famous disputes, how to search each office, the classes, and descriptive names

### Takeaway
Game-name disputes usually come from an owner of a famous or broadly claimed mark (ZeniMax with "Scrolls", King with "Candy" and "Saga") or from a trademark troll (Tim Langdell with "Edge"). Small studios often pay in delay and rebranding even when they're right. Screen with the free official databases: the USPTO's Trademark Search (TESS was retired on 30 November 2023), EUIPO's eSearch plus and TMview, WIPO's Global Brand Database, and Israel's database, which feeds TMview and the Global Brand Database. Look at classes 9 (downloadable game software), 41 (online game and entertainment services) and 28 (physical games and toys). Descriptive words such as "Evolution" and "Shop" make a mark weak and hard to protect. In the US, a single game's title can be refused as the title of a single creative work until there's a series.

### Cited Findings
**Disputes and what they cost**
- **Scrolls (ZeniMax/Bethesda v. Mojang, 2011–2012):** ZeniMax objected that Mojang's "Scrolls" resembled its Elder Scrolls mark. Mojang won an interim injunction in Sweden in October 2011. The March 2012 settlement let Mojang keep the name for the game, but Mojang gave up the Scrolls trademark, with no damages paid. A Bethesda VP said the company was "forced" to defend its mark — [PC Gamer: Mojang wins interim injunction](https://www.pcgamer.com/mojang-wins-interim-injunction-for-scrolls-trademark); [GamesRadar: Mojang and ZeniMax settled](https://www.gamesradar.com/mojang-and-zenimax-settled-scrolls-dispute); [Techdirt, 2012](https://archive.techdirt.com/articles/20120312/10553618078/mojang-bethesda-reach-settlement-scrolls-trademark-dispute.shtml); [Shacknews](https://shacknews.com/article/70509/legal-experts-bethesda-explain-scrolls-dispute)
- **Candy (King, 2013–2014):** King won a trademark on the word "Candy" for video games in the EU and sent cease-and-desist letters to games with "Candy" in the title. Indies answered with the "Candy Jam". King filed its US application in February 2013 (serial 85/842,584) and abandoned it on 24 February 2014, after buying the earlier rights to "Candy Crusher". It kept the EU "Candy" mark — [Game Informer, Feb 2014](https://gameinformer.com/b/news/archive/2014/02/25/crushed-by-bad-press-king-abandons-candy-trademark); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2014-02-25-king-withdraws-candy-trademark-attempt.html); [Finnegan (law firm blog)](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/blogs/incontestable/amid-bitter-backlash-company-abandons-efforts-to-trademark-candy.html); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2014/02/26/king_pulls_us_candy_trademark_attempt/)
- **Saga (King v. Stoic, The Banner Saga, 2013–2014):** King filed a Notice of Opposition on 27 December 2013 against Stoic's US application for "The Banner Saga", calling it confusingly similar to King's "Saga" marks (Candy Crush Saga, Farm Heroes Saga, Pet Rescue Saga), although King had no registration for "Saga" alone. Stoic said it filed first, and that the dispute put sequel plans on hold. Reports said King argued it had to oppose consistently or risk looking like it had abandoned its marks — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/video-games/first-candy-now-saga-candy-crush-saga-goes-after-indies-n14741); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-banner-saga-3); [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/indie/king-trademark-dispute-threatens-banner-saga-sequel-we-wont-make-viking-saga-without-word-saga)
- **Edge (Tim Langdell / Edge Games):** Langdell claimed Mobigame's iPhone game "Edge" infringed his marks. Mobigame pulled it, and the renamed "Edge by Mobigame" was removed again. Langdell sued EA over Mirror's Edge in June 2010. EA had already abandoned its own "Mirror's Edge" application (8 September 2009) and petitioned to cancel Langdell's marks. A US court denied Langdell an injunction in October 2010, describing his conduct as trolling and finding his evidence fraudulent. The USPTO issued the cancellation of his "Edge" registrations on 17 April 2013 — [Engadget, 2010](https://www.engadget.com/2010-10-05-ea-wins-edge-trademark-suit-court-cites-langdell-for-trollin.html); [Engadget, 2013](https://www.engadget.com/2013-04-19-langdells-edge-trademark-canceled-by-court-order.html); [MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/court-breaks-langdell-grip-on-edge-trademark/); [Pocket Gamer](https://www.pocketgamer.com/mirrors-edge/langdell-stripped-of-edge-trademarks-could-face-charges/)
- **Steam's process acknowledges legal renames:** Valve's form for renaming after review asks "Is there anything about the existing game name that you need to change for legal reasons?" — [Steamworks: Changing your game's name](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/editing/name)

**Where to search, and how**
- **USPTO (United States):** TESS was retired on 30 November 2023 and replaced by the cloud-based Trademark Search at tmsearch.uspto.gov, which uses a new query syntax. The USPTO handout gives examples such as `cat[comb]` and `CM:cat`, the latter for phonetic equivalents. Practitioners say it is more case sensitive than TESS, and full records open through Trademark Document Retrieval (TSDR/TDR) — [USPTO: Retiring TESS](https://www.uspto.gov/subscription-center/2023/retiring-tess-what-know-about-new-trademark-search-system); [USPTO handout: Transitioning from TESS](https://USPto.gov/sites/default/files/documents/TM-Transitioning-from-TESS-handout.pdf); [Alt Legal](https://www.altlegal.com/blog/about-the-usptos-new-trademark-search-system/)
- **EUIPO (European Union):** eSearch plus covers EU trade mark applications and registrations and international registrations that designate the EU, free of charge. TMview, built by EUIPO, searches 75+ offices free of charge, and one guide calls it the largest free trademark database. Recommended workflow: search the exact mark, then phonetic equivalents, spelling variants and translations into the target markets' languages, then confirm on each priority national register — [Intepat: TMview](https://www.intepat.com/blog/trademark/tmview/); [Trama TM: How to use eSearch](https://www.tramatm.com/trademark-questions-and-answers/specifics-of-eu-trademarks/how-do-i-use-the-euipo-esearch-tool); [Trademark Factory: EUIPO search tools](https://trademarkfactory.com/euipos-trademark-search-tools-and-databases)
- **WIPO Global Brand Database:** free, more than 60 million records from 74+ offices. Searches by brand, holder, number or image. Madrid-system records are uploaded automatically and may lag. Reports are limited to 180 downloaded records — [WIPO Global Brand Database FAQ](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/global-brand-database/faqs_branddb); [Intepat](https://www.intepat.com/blog/eu-trademark-search-euipo)
- **Israel (ILPO, Israel Patent Office, Trademarks Department):** a public trademark database searches registered and pending marks by various criteria, with application history, documents, status and legal proceedings. ILPO sends trademark data weekly to WIPO's Global Brand Database and to EUIPO's TMview. The department's page is on gov.il; an older justice.gov.il search address from 2013 may be stale — [WIPO: ILPO Annual Technical Report on Trademarks 2024](https://confluence.wipo.int/confluence/spaces/ATR/pages/1640669323/CWS+ATR+TM+2024+IL); [gov.il Trademarks Department](https://www.gov.il/en/departments/topics/trademark-department/govil-landing-page); [Latvian patent office: Israel joins TMview](https://www.lrpv.gov.lv/en/article/israel-joins-tmview)

**Classes and registrability**
- Class 9 is the usual class for a downloadable game sold on Steam and the app stores. Class 41 covers online games and entertainment services, for example "providing online multiplayer video games". Class 28 covers games and toys, and matters mostly for physical products or merchandise. Some offices and filers treat video games under class 28 with class 9 as related — [Promise Legal: Trademark registration for game studios](https://blog.promise.legal/trademark-registration-game-studios/); [IncorpX: class 28, video game](https://www.incorpx.io/trademark-class-28/video-game)
- **Single-work titles (US):** TMEP § 1202.08 says the title of a single creative work isn't registrable on either register. A series title can be, once used on at least two works. In Mattel v. Brainy Baby, the TTAB required a title to be used on at least two of the applicant's works. In "STRONGHOLDS & FOLLOWERS" (TTAB, precedential), a role-playing game manual's title failed to function as a mark. One law-firm blog says a standalone game with no sequels or DLC may face a single-work refusal, and suggests identifying the goods as "downloadable video game software" and registering the studio name first — [Patent Trademark Blog: How to trademark creative works](https://www.patenttrademarkblog.com/how-to-trademark-books-dvds-creative-works/); [World Trademark Review: Title of single work not registrable](https://www.worldtrademarkreview.com/article/title-of-single-work-not-registrable); [vLex: Stronghold & Followers](https://vlex.com/vid/precedential-no-6-title-899027363); [Promise Legal](https://blog.promise.legal/trademark-registration-game-studios/)
- **Descriptiveness abroad:** Thailand's trademark registrar and Board of Trademarks have treated a game title as descriptive of a computer game, which was the basis of the Clash Royale appeal — search summary of [Tilleke & Gibbins insights archive](https://tilleke.com/insights-archive/page/78) (page not opened)

### Inferences
- **Procedure: trademark screening** (screening only; it doesn't replace an attorney's clearance):
  1. For each candidate, search the exact word mark and each distinctive word alone in: the USPTO Trademark Search (tmsearch.uspto.gov), EUIPO eSearch plus, TMview, WIPO's Global Brand Database, and Israel's database. TMview and the Global Brand Database include Israel, but confirm on gov.il.
  2. Repeat with phonetic equivalents (the USPTO's CM: operator), spelling variants, plurals, and the name with spaces removed or joined.
  3. Filter to classes 9, 41 and 28, then look at live marks only, but note recently dead marks of active game companies.
  4. **Conflict, in order of severity:**
     - (a) A live mark identical or near-identical in class 9 or 41: stop.
     - (b) A live mark sharing the distinctive word in class 9, 41 or 28, held by a games company, especially a large one that polices its marks (King, ZeniMax): high risk, as with Scrolls and Saga.
     - (c) A famous mark in any class: dilution risk.
     - (d) Unregistered prior use by an existing game, found in section 2: common-law and passing-off risk in the US, Israel and the UK.
  5. Note which words are descriptive or generic for the goods. They can't anchor protection, and an owner of a similar descriptive mark has weak rights too.
- **"Evolution Shop" under this check:**
  - "Evolution" describes the game's theme (creatures evolving), and "Shop" describes its genre (shop management). A descriptive phrase like this is likely weak: hard to register without proof of acquired distinctiveness, and hard to enforce against others.
  - Meanwhile "Evolution" is likely already registered by others in classes 9, 28 and 41. Many brands use it, and the "Evolution" board game is one possible example (unverified). So it gets the worst of both: little protection of its own, and a risk from others' existing marks.
  - This inference follows the general US "merely descriptive" doctrine (TMEP § 1209), which I didn't open in this session; see Gaps.
- Even a won dispute costs an indie time and money (Stoic's sequel was put on hold; Mobigame's game was pulled twice). For a small studio, avoiding a crowded word matters more than winning.

### Gaps
- I didn't open TMEP § 1209 (merely descriptive marks) or EUIPO's guidelines on descriptiveness (EUTMR Article 7(1)(b) and (c)) in this session. The descriptiveness inference rests on general trademark doctrine, not on a fetched source.
- How The Banner Saga opposition ended wasn't in the sources found; check TTABVUE.
- After the settlement, Mojang's "Scrolls" was reportedly re-released under another name ("Caller's Bane", 2018). That's from memory and unverified here; if confirmed, it's a relevant case of a game that had to stop using its name.
- Israeli trademark law specifics (the Trade Marks Ordinance's rules on descriptive marks, and the Israeli class practice for games) weren't researched. The exact current URL of ILPO's trademark search wasn't confirmed.
- I didn't search any office for "Evolution" or "Evolution Shop" marks in this session.

## 4. International use: pronounceability, meanings in other languages, localised titles, and Hebrew speakers

### Takeaway
Names that non-native speakers can't say or spell hurt word of mouth and search. Valheim was reportedly renamed from "Fejd" partly because overseas players couldn't pronounce it, and Stardew Valley's first name was "not pleasant to say". Translators generally keep the English title as the brand and localise the text, sometimes adding a native-script subtitle. Steam lets developers add localised app names, through support once the page has been reviewed. I found no documented guidance specific to Hebrew speakers.

### Cited Findings
- Valheim began in 2017 under the working title "Fejd" (Swedish for "feud") and was renamed in 2018. A Japanese report on an Iron Gate developer talk says the change was because overseas players had trouble pronouncing "Fejd"; I couldn't confirm that in English — [Wikipedia: Valheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valheim); [Game*Spark (Japanese), Feb 2022](https://www.gamespark.jp/article/2022/02/10/115842.html)
- Stardew Valley's original name, Sprout Valley, was replaced partly because the word "Sprout" was hard or "not pleasant to say" — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/stardew-valley-creator-says-the-games-name-just-came-to-me-after-the-original-was-not-pleasant-to-say)
- The Game Developer guide's test is "nice rhythm when said out loud, in English", with the counter-example "Try to say Rymdkapsel?" — [Game Developer: How to name a game](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-to-name-a-game)
- Proper-noun titles usually stay in English while the interface is localised (Stardew Valley is the example). Pokémon is the counter-example, with fully translated titles (Sword and Shield become Épée and Bouclier). Advice includes a native-script subtitle in key markets, checking with native speakers, and avoiding literal translations that can be misread — [Level Up Translation: Should you localize your game's title?](https://www.leveluptranslation.com/single-post/should-you-localize-your-games-title); [Game Developer: Should you localize your game's title?](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/should-you-localize-your-game-s-title-); [Unreal Engine forums: localising a title for Japan and China](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/localization-strategy-should-we-translate-the-core-title-of-an-fmv-game-e-g-japanese-chinese-markets/2676323)
- A study on translating Chinese game titles argues that players search for reviews and discussion by the title, so a clear localised title matters for findability — search summary of [Universidad de Málaga repository (RIUMA)](https://riuma.uma.es/entities/publication/72744a72-5bc7-406e-8367-ae86605e0e59) (not opened)
- Steam supports localised app names. After pre-release review, adding one needs Steamworks support — [Steamworks: Changing your game's name](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/editing/name)
- Check acronyms and readings in other languages (the "Light Of Lamps" acronym collides with LoL) — search summary of [FCHQ](https://fchq.io/news/tips-to-creating-a-game-name) / [Buildbox](https://www.buildbox.com/how-to-name-a-game-in-7-steps)

### Inferences
- **Procedure: international check.**
  1. Have speakers of the big Steam languages (at least Simplified Chinese, Russian, Spanish, Portuguese, German, French, Japanese and Korean), plus Hebrew, read the name aloud and say what it sounds like or means.
  2. Run the name and each word through a dictionary and slang search (Urban Dictionary and native-speaker review) in those languages.
  3. Check the acronym.
  4. Check the name has no letters or clusters that are hard across languages (th, w/v, silent letters, unusual vowel spellings).
  - **Conflict:** an offensive or comic meaning, a near-homophone of something unwanted, a name native speakers can't say, or, in a market you'll localise for, a common local word that would bury it in search.
- **Hebrew-speaker notes** (reasoning only; no source found): the developer and first players are Israeli.
  - A name will usually be written in Latin letters and spoken with Hebrew phonology. Sounds like "th", "w" and the distinction between short vowels don't come easily.
  - An English word that is also a Hebrew word or slang, or that sounds like one, could read oddly at home.
  - Transliteration back into Hebrew script (for press, word of mouth, or a Hebrew subtitle) should not produce an unfortunate Hebrew word.
- **"Evolution Shop" under this check:** "Evolution" is an international loanword, close in most European languages, and in Hebrew the related word is אבולוציה. It's easy to understand but is translated in some markets (Chinese 进化, Japanese 進化 or エボリューション). A localised title would then be a common word in that language too, adding to the search problem.

### Gaps
- No documented source on Hebrew-speaker naming considerations, Israeli players' search habits, or whether Steam offers a Hebrew store interface (needs checking on Steam's list of supported languages).
- No data on how often localised titles help or hurt sales.

## 5. Renaming late in development, before or after a Steam page exists

### Takeaway
On Steam the name is free to change until the store page passes pre-release review. After that, changing it requires Profile Features to be unlocked and a request to Steamworks support explaining why, whether it's for legal reasons, and whether the product is changing. The app ID stays the same, so the page's wishlists stay with the app, though Valve doesn't document this directly. The real costs are confusion, lost search equity (old coverage, videos and links under the old name) and re-doing the marketing. Many successful games were renamed in development (Stardew Valley, Valheim, Rocket League's relaunch, Steredenn, Gunkid 99, Ministry of Order).

### Cited Findings
- Steam name changes are free "at any time until your store page has been through the pre-release review process". After that "you won't be able to change the name unless you have unlocked Profile Features", and you contact Steamworks support, answering: "Why is this name change necessary?", "Is there anything about the existing game name that you need to change for legal reasons?", and "Is the product that was advertised to customers changing along with the name?". The name is set in three places (Store, Steamworks settings, Packages), and all three must change — [Steamworks: Changing your game's name](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/editing/name)
- A developer reported that with a Steam page you "can still change the name of your game easily enough", but "if you've already released a demo, you'd have to contact Steamworks developer support to change the name of your demo" — [Gunkid 99 devlog](https://yongjustyong.itch.io/gunkid99/devlog/48419/gunkid-99-previously-99999-name-change-and-updates)
- A Steam App ID is permanent and doesn't change when the game is renamed in Steamworks. A vendor guide advises settling the name before the Coming Soon page gets significant traffic — [Steam Page Analyzer: How to get a Steam App ID](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/how-to-get-steam-app-id) (vendor blog, secondary)
- A Steam Community thread notes wishlists are tracked by app ID, so a wishlisted app persists through changes (and shows as "unavailable" if removed) — [Steam Community thread](https://steamcommunity.com/discussions/forum/1/7074686732306817122) (community source; Valve gives no official statement)
- **Rocket League:** its predecessor, "Supersonic Acrobatic Rocket-Powered Battle-Cars" (PS3, 2008), self-published "with almost no marketing", was downloaded over two million times but "was not considered very successful". For the sequel, the name was chosen partly "to reduce the size of the game's title in order to appear fully in digital storefronts" and to sound more mature. Design manager Corey Davis called Battle-Cars "the worst game name of all time". (This was a new game with a new name, not a store rename) — [Wikipedia: Rocket League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_League)
- **Stardew Valley** was "Sprout Valley" from 2012, renamed before release; **Valheim** was "Fejd" (2017), renamed in 2018 — [Nintendo Everything](https://nintendoeverything.com/stardew-valley-was-originally-called-sprout-valley-plus-other-development-changes/); [Wikipedia: Valheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valheim)
- **Ministry of Order** (formerly "Diapers, Please!") was renamed by the time its Steam page went live, to avoid resembling Papers, Please — [VoltekPlay devlog](https://voltekplay.itch.io/diapers-please/devlog/901651/big-news-our-steam-page-is-now-live)
- Trademark-forced renames after release are costly: Mobigame's "Edge" was pulled from the App Store, and the renamed version was pulled again — [MCV/Develop](https://www.mcvuk.com/business-news/publishing/mobigame-to-pull-edgy-amid-ea-legal-fears/)

### Inferences
- **Cheapest moment:** before the Steam page is submitted for review, and before trailers, a demo, festival entries (Next Fest), press or a subreddit exist under the name. For "Evolution Shop", if no Steam page has passed review yet, renaming now costs almost nothing on Steam.
- **Procedure: before you commit a name to a Steam page:**
  1. Pass the checks in sections 1 to 4.
  2. Reserve the handles and domain the same day.
  3. Search the trademark offices again just before submitting.
  4. Consider filing at least a class 9 application, or registering the studio name, once committed. In the US, the single-work rule may mean the title only registers once there's a series or extra content.
- **After a rename**, keep the old name in the store description ("formerly X") and in your channels for a while so old links and searches still lead to the game. That's practice seen in the devlogs, not a Valve requirement.

### Gaps
- Valve doesn't document whether a renamed app's search keeps matching the old name, whether the URL slug updates, or whether followers and wishlisters are notified. I found no official statement on wishlists surviving a rename beyond the app-ID reasoning and a community thread.
- I found no evidence that Slay the Spire or Hollow Knight were renamed. The assignment named them as possible examples, but I couldn't confirm any earlier titles in this session.
- No figures on the cost of renaming (fees or lost wishlists). Valve lists no fee for a rename.

## 6. Formats: subtitles ("Name: The X Game") and descriptive suffixes ("... Simulator", "... Shop")

### Takeaway
I found no evidence that a subtitle or genre suffix improves Steam search ranking. The evidence points the other way on length: Psyonix shortened its title to fit storefronts, and Apple caps app names at 30 characters and forbids keyword padding. Naming advice favours a distinctive core word plus a word that signals the game. A genre word as a suffix ("Simulator", "Shop") tells shoppers what the game is, which consultants stress, but it adds no distinctiveness and no trademark strength.

### Cited Findings
- Rocket League's name was chosen partly "to reduce the size of the game's title in order to appear fully in digital storefronts" — [Wikipedia: Rocket League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_League)
- Apple guideline 2.3.7: app names must be unique and limited to 30 characters. They should not include prices, terms or descriptions that are not the name of the app, or keyword-stuffed or trademarked terms — [Apple Developer Forums quoting 2.3.7](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/124046); [Phiture](https://phiture.com/asostack/app-store-introduces-50-character-limit-of-app-names-138477cbf92b/)
- Pairing a generic word with an original word (Drifting Lands), or longer structures, helps a name stand out. The name should tie to the gameplay, as with Hatoful Boyfriend and Clash of Clans — [Game Developer: How to name a game](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-to-name-a-game)
- Gunkid 99 paired a rare invented word with a number to be "more easily searchable" — [Gunkid 99 devlog](https://yongjustyong.itch.io/gunkid99/devlog/48419/gunkid-99-previously-99999-name-change-and-updates)
- Chris Zukowski (howtomarketagame.com) stresses that on a Steam page "clarity is king", so a shopper should instantly tell what kind of game it is — [Unity blog: 5 tips & strategies for marketing indie games](https://unity.com/blog/tips-strategies-for-marketing-indie-games)
- Since 2023, Steam's search suggests tags that match typed words. A title word that is also a tag name shares autocomplete with that tag and every game using the word — [GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2023/05/valve-gave-steam-store-search-a-very-useful-upgrade)
- A native-script or descriptive subtitle can serve new players in foreign markets — [Level Up Translation](https://www.leveluptranslation.com/single-post/should-you-localize-your-games-title)

### Inferences
- **For screening:**
  - A subtitle or suffix is fine as a clarity aid if the core word passes the uniqueness and trademark checks on its own, since people will shorten the name to the core word in speech, hashtags and handles.
  - A name that is only genre words (like "Evolution Shop") fails, because it has no core word to own.
  - A long title also risks being cut off in Steam capsules, the library and mobile stores.
- **For "Evolution Shop":** it's essentially a descriptive pair with no distinctive core. A suffix such as "Simulator" would add clarity but not findability or protection.

### Gaps
- No A/B data or Valve statement on whether "Simulator", "Tycoon" or "Shop" in a title improves search ranking or conversion on Steam. The popularity of "X Simulator" titles is anecdotal and wasn't sourced here.
- Steam's exact character limits for store names and where names get cut off weren't checked.
