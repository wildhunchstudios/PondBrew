# Marketing and linguistic principles for naming a small indie Steam game

Scope: what makes a video game title work commercially and psychologically, with a focus on small indie PC games on Steam, and on what matters for a cosy, first-person shop-and-lab sim about hatching, keeping, selling, and breeding small aquatic creatures (working title "Evolution Shop"). Each principle is written as a usable test ("A good name ...") with its source and an example. Sources from 2015 to 2026 unless marked **[older]**. Researched 2026-11-03.

Context for weighing all of this: a name is a small lever. GameDiscoverCo's word data shows that "almost all of the median revenue numbers are worryingly low" ([GameDiscoverCo, 2023](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/whats-in-a-name-top-steam-game-titles)), and third-party estimates say most Steam games earn little whatever they're called (Gamalytic: 67% of Steam games under $5,000 lifetime; Video Game Insights: only 5% of 2023 releases over $100,000), as reported by [Game World Observer, 2026](https://gameworldobserver.com/2026/09/01/the-median-revenue-of-games-on-steam-has-been-declining-for-12-consecutive-years) and [WN Hub](https://wnhub.io/news/stores-and-publishing/item-42136). These are estimates; Valve publishes no sales data.

## 1. What do indie game marketing experts recommend about naming?

### Takeaway
The most-cited practical advice (Chris Zukowski, howtomarketagame.com) is that a Steam name's job is to be **said, heard, spelled and searched without friction** and to **hint at the genre** within the seconds a shopper spends scanning a list; SEO and length barely matter. Developer post-mortems add: avoid bland, misleading, obscure, controversial, or easily forgotten names, test them aloud on strangers, and check for similar existing names early.

### Cited Findings
**Chris Zukowski, "Are game names getting longer..." (howtomarketagame.com, 3 Feb 2025), based on 94,371 Steam games released 2016 to 2025:**
- **Test: a good name can be understood when shouted across a noisy convention floor.** Zukowski: "Name your game something that can be understood when shouted across a noisy convention floor." His test: say the title somewhere noisy, have someone write down what they heard, then ask what genre they think it is. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Test: a fan can pass the name on by voice.** "If a fan of your game has to write down your game's name so their friend can find it, that is bad." — [howtomarketagame.com](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464) (quoted in search-tool summary of the site; the fetched article carried the same advice)
- **Test: a streamer can say it correctly without thinking, and a viewer can then type it into Steam.** "You need the youtuber to be able to say your game's name correctly without thinking about it." Word of mouth and YouTube are the main channels where names travel. — [howtomarketagame.com](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Test: a good name avoids invented words and apostrophe-heavy spellings that can't be spelled after hearing them.** — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Test: a good name, with the capsule art and tags, hints at the genre.** "Valve gives you just a few places where you can hint at your genre, so you need to be smart about it." And: "Ironically, I think you don't want your title to be so unique that nobody knows what it is." He also warns against chains of generic words that blur together. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **SEO matters little.** Google and Steam search find games by name; even single-character titles came up first in a simple search. Only emoji-only names are a problem: "as long as you aren't naming your game a combination of emoji characters, you will be fine." Most shoppers find games through festivals, streamers, Reddit and social media, not genre keyword searches. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Length does not correlate with sales.** Asked whether longer titles sell better: "No, no it does not." Six single-character titles since 2016 didn't help sales either. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- His broader marketing plan says the job of all messaging is to "tell people what genre your game is and what makes your game unique", and to test messaging on strangers who play games by asking which games it reminds them of. — [howtomarketagame.com, 10-step plan, 2021, updated 2024](https://howtomarketagame.com/2021/07/12/how-to-market-your-indie-game-a-10-step-plan/)

**Gerald Fitzsimons, "How the wrong name can kill your game" (Game Developer, 10 Nov 2016), from renaming his own language-learning shooter:**
- **Test: a good name creates interest in the target audience, not just in its maker.** "pick a name that creates interest in the mind of your target audience"; "Make it automatically interesting." "Good is not good enough if you don't have a $30 million dollar marketing budget." — [Game Developer, 2016](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Test: a good name isn't bland.** "Galaxy Rescue" suggested "nothing or a top-down arcade space shooter." — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Test: a good name doesn't promise a different game.** His hypothetical "Word Challenge" would read as a crossword game for grandfathers, not an alien shooter. — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Test: a good name uses words the audience knows.** "Prattle Battle" sounded too tame and many people don't know "prattle". — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Test: a good name is safe to share, especially for mixed-age audiences.** "if it's a game for adults and children then controversy is not your friend"; "With a $0 budget you need people to not worry about sharing it." "Slaves' Sunrise" upset American testers. — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Caution on testing:** in a blind test 90% picked "Slaves' Sunrise", which he later judged wrong: it suited a GTA-style game better, and European and Asian testers read "slave" differently from Americans. His final name, "World Word War", borrowed familiarity from world war games. — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)

**Renaming case studies, John Polson, Game Developer (3 Oct 2011) [older]:**
- **Test: a good name works both said and read.** Alexander Bruce changed "Antechamber" to "Antichamber" because it "worked better when I said it than when people read it, because they were hearing me say 'anti-chamber'." He also dropped "Hazard: The Journey of Life" as a name chosen "for lack of time or forethought." — [Game Developer, 2011](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)
- **Test: a good name describes something true about the game.** Dejobaan dropped "ooo! ooO! oOO! OOO!: Grab a Loop and Mix a Beat" partly because it "wasn't descriptive (there was no mixing of beats)" and was too close to their previous game. — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)
- **Test: a good name isn't phonetically close to an existing game.** "Icarian" became "NyxQuest" two days before release after a cease and desist over a name "similar phonetically". "Retro Theftendo" became "Retro City Rampage" to avoid "-tendo". Advice: "be prepared and not very attached to a name"; more than three renames "turns it into a game of its own." — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)

**Other practitioner advice:**
- **Test: a good name is memorable to its own team.** The cosy creature game "Mystic Village" was renamed "Gourdlets" because the developer "kept forgetting it, which didn't seem like a great sign." — [aunty games devlog, itch.io, c. 2022](https://aunty-games.itch.io/gourdlets/devlog/385478/new-title-)
- Wouter Walmink chose his game's name with a small, cheap player survey and reports ending up not with the name he liked best but with a better one. — [Medium, Walmink](https://medium.com/@wouter_walmink/picking-the-right-name-for-your-indie-game-f03cd7bfa763) (page returned 403; known only from a search snippet, so method and numbers are unverified)
- A legal-focused guide warns that domain or social-handle availability is not proof a name is free; check trademarks. — [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/how-to-name-video-game-legal-licensing-indie-devs) (from search snippet only)

**Steam's own rules (Steamworks documentation):**
- Base store capsules may show only "game artwork, the game name, and any official subtitle"; library capsules must show the logo and may add the subtitle, "No other text". Breaking this can limit visibility. So the name (and an optional official subtitle) is the only text a game gets on its capsule. — [Steamworks: Graphical asset rules](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/assets/rules)
- The name can be changed freely until the store page has passed pre-release review, and is set in three places (store, library, package) that should match. Localised names are optional and shown by the player's language. — [Steamworks: Editing your game's name](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/editing/name)

### Inferences
- The experts agree on a two-part job: **fluency** (say, hear, spell, remember, search) and **positioning** (genre and what's special). No expert source argues that a clever or "beautiful" name compensates for failing either.
- Because the capsule may carry only the name and an official subtitle, the name plus the capsule art carry almost the whole first impression in Steam lists; a name that leaves genre ambiguous has to be rescued by the art.
- For a first-person creature shop sim whose audience will hear about it from cosy streamers and TikTok, the spoken tests (noisy floor, streamer, word of mouth) are more important than written-search tests.

### Gaps
- I found no naming-specific GDC talk, no Jesse Schell or Mike Rose writing on naming, and no naming posts from Raw Fury, Devolver or Fellow Traveller in this session. Searches turned up only blog posts and forum threads.
- I found no wishlist A/B data comparing names for the same game (Steam doesn't allow name A/B tests; the Walmink survey could not be read).
- Igor's own naming guide (PDF) could not be read here; see section 2 for what secondary sources say about it.

## 2. Should a name signal genre, tone and core fantasy ("tells you what you do") or be evocative/abstract? When does each work?

### Takeaway
On Steam, an indie name works best when it **points at the genre or core fantasy without being a generic genre label**: either a descriptive name with a distinctive twist, or an evocative name backed by unmistakable capsule art. Pure descriptive names blend into competitors; pure abstract names leave shoppers guessing. Naming a creature game after its creatures is a proven cosy-genre move.

### Cited Findings
- **Test: a good name, together with the capsule and tags, lets a stranger guess the genre.** Zukowski: shoppers scan quickly, the title is one of the few places to hint at genre, and "you don't want your title to be so unique that nobody knows what it is." — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Test: a good name isn't so generic that it could be anyone's game.** Fitzsimons: "Galaxy Rescue" suggested nothing specific. — [Game Developer, 2016](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- The Igor naming agency sorts names into functional/descriptive, invented, experiential, and evocative. Its guide says descriptive company names tend to blend together with competitors' names. — [Igor guide, Scribd copy (short version)](https://www.scribd.com/doc/93627699/Igor-Naming-Guide-Short); [Igor's site](https://www.igorinternational.com/llms.txt). Secondary sources say Igor calls evocative names the most successful category and the hardest to get right (Apple, Virgin). — [nojoeschmo.com](https://nojoeschmo.com/tag/branding/). **Conflict:** Wikipedia describes Igor's "almost militant embrace" of real words and its advice against invented words — [Wikiwand: Igor Naming Agency](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Igor_Naming_Agency) — while Igor's current site lists invented names as a standard category. (I could not read the guide itself; summary is from search snippets.)
- Root-based invented names (Agilent, Acquient) are described as less memorable than invented names with a simple sound (Oreo, Google). — [Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/money-finance/the-secret-to-a-good-business-name/218458) (search snippet)
- **Test (cosy creature games): a good name can be the creatures themselves.** The developer of "Gourdlets" wanted the name to reflect the game's little creatures, who needed "a more unique name than" spirits or villagers; after "seedlings" and "Plant Archipelago?", chose a real word plus a diminutive: "gourd" is a great word and "gourdlets" sounds cute. — [aunty games devlog](https://aunty-games.itch.io/gourdlets/devlog/385478/new-title-)
- **Test: a plainly descriptive name works when the brand already carries recognition.** Sunblink said "Hello Kitty Island Adventure" was chosen because the game "has always been an island adventure" (the IP does the evocative work). — [Vice interview with Logan Beru](https://www.vice.com/en/article/logan-beru-director-of-marketing-dishes-on-hello-kitty-island-adventure-and-how-it-became-a-cozy-game-icon-interview/)
- **Evidence an evocative premise name can beat a genre-label name in the first-person sim space:** GameDiscoverCo notes "Roadside Research" (aliens in disguise running a gas station) does not use "Simulator" in its name, yet reached 258,457 wishlists after its reveal and demo (Sept 2025), while 38 games with "Simulator" in the title launched on Steam in Aug 2025 and only one (Waterpark Simulator) had passed about $70k gross. — [GameDiscoverCo, 23 Sept 2025](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-rise-of-the-first-person-sim)
- **Test: a good name says something true about the game** (Dejobaan's "OOO!" rename because it "wasn't descriptive"). — [Game Developer, 2011 [older]](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)

### Inferences
- Descriptive works when the genre label is itself the hook and the audience searches for it (management games: "Manager", "Tycoon"; see section 4). Evocative works when the capsule, trailer and streamers can show the genre instantly and the name supplies personality and ownability (e.g., Roadside Research).
- For a cosy creature game, a middle path fits the evidence: a **real or near-real word that names the creatures, the place, or the act** (Gourdlets pattern), so the name is both specific to this game and hints at the fantasy (small creatures, water, hatching, a shop).
- "Evolution Shop" is two generic dictionary words, both functional; by the Igor and Fitzsimons tests it risks blending with others and doesn't carry the core fantasy (aquatic creatures, hatching, cosiness). "Evolution" alone may suggest biology or strategy games rather than cute creature-keeping. This is my inference, not tested with players.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study compares descriptive versus evocative indie game names on Steam outcomes.
- I could not open Igor's full guide, so its exact criteria and examples are unverified here.

## 3. How much do length, word count, pronounceability, spelling, invented words, sound symbolism, alliteration and rhythm matter?

### Takeaway
On Steam, **length itself doesn't predict sales**, but **pronounceability and spellability matter** for word of mouth, and psychology research shows easy-to-say names get a fluency advantage. Sound symbolism is real but small and partly language-specific: in English, **front vowels and bilabials (p, b, m) read smaller, softer, cuter and friendlier; voiced stops (d, g) and longer names read bigger, stronger, more evolved or villainous**. Sound repetition (alliteration, rhyme) helps when heard, but too much backfires.

### Cited Findings
**Length and word count**
- Across 94,371 Steam games (2016 to 2025), title length does not correlate with sales; single-character titles didn't help either. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)

**Pronounceability and fluency**
- **Test: a good name is easy to pronounce on first sight.** Alter & Oppenheimer (PNAS 2006) **[older]**: lab participants judged invented names like "Hillard" or "Barning" fluent and "Xagibdan" or "Creaumy" complex; in real NYSE/AMEX data 1990 to 2004, companies with easier-to-pronounce names and tickers got a bigger short-term bump after listing ($1,000 in pronounceable tickers netted $85.35 more after one day), controlling for size, country and industry. The effect was strongest shortly after IPO and is not a long-run predictor. — [Princeton news, 2006](https://www.princeton.edu/news/2006/05/29/study-stock-performance-tied-ease-pronouncing-companys-name); [ScienceDaily, 2006](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/05/060530083959.htm)
- **Test: a good invented or respelled word is spelled the way it sounds.** Zukowski warns against invented words and apostrophes people can't spell after hearing them. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Test: say it to someone who hasn't seen it written.** Antechamber to Antichamber. — [Game Developer, 2011 [older]](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)

**Sound symbolism: vowels and consonants (brand research)**
- Klink (Marketing Letters, 2000) **[older]**: brand names with front vowels (as in "bee", "bit") were rated smaller, softer and prettier (and colder, faster, more bitter) than back-vowel names (as in "boot", "boat"). A 2021 review notes that "some studies do not support universal sound symbolism", and the authors' own Japanese results were inconsistent with Klink. — [Frontiers in Communication, 2021 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2021.617532/full)
- Secondary summaries of Klink also report fricatives (f, s, v, z) rated smaller, faster and lighter than stops, and voiceless stops lighter than voiced ones; not verified against the original here. — [search summary pointing to the sceco.ub.ro review](https://sceco.ub.ro/index.php/SCECO/article/download/304/294/958)
- **Conflicting small studies:** a 2015 Romanian study (59 students) found back-vowel brand names rated better on brand performance, with no effect for stops versus fricatives — [SCECO, 2015](https://sceco.ub.ro/SCECO/article/view/275); a 2015 Korean study found most consonants, especially fricatives, more symbolic at the start of a name, and /k/ more at the end — [earticle.net](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A258395). These are small samples; treat as weak.
- Bouba/kiki: pseudowords like "maluma"/"bouba" are associated with round shapes, "takete"/"kiki" with pointy ones. — [Frontiers in Communication, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2021.617532/full)

**Sound symbolism for creature names (the most relevant evidence for this game)**
- **Test: a cute, friendly creature-game name leans on bilabials (p, b, m), not voiced stops.** Kilpatrick, Ćwiek, Lewis & Kawahara (Frontiers in Psychology, 2023) analysed 898 Pokémon in six languages (5,388 names): "Bilabial plosives, /p/ and /b/, typically represent high friendship values in Pokémon names", while /d/, /g/ and /m/ "typically represent low friendship values"; low-friendship Pokémon are "significantly heavier and stronger." Caveats: in the borrowing-controlled model /b/ was only marginal (p = 0.063) and /m/ not significant. — [Frontiers in Psychology, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1113143/full)
- Prior work cited there: Kawahara et al. (Phonetica, 2018): in Japanese Pokémon names, voiced obstruents (b, d, g, z) and name length track size, weight, strength and evolution level; Shih et al. (2018): longer names represent "increased size, power, and evolution level" in Japanese and English; Uno et al. (2020): voiced obstruents go with villains, bilabials with non-villains; Kumagai & Kawahara (2017) and Kumagai (2020): "bilabial consonants are related to cuteness in English and Japanese." — [Frontiers in Psychology, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1113143/full); [Kawahara et al. 2018, lingbuzz](https://lingbuzz.net/lingbuzz/003196/current.pdf)
- Japanese research also links singleton [p] to cuteness more than other consonants. — [Journal of Japanese Linguistics paper via OUCI](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4EMM3yx4) (author not shown in listing)

**Repetition, alliteration, rhythm**
- **Test: a good name may repeat a sound, heard aloud, but not overdo it.** Argo, Popa & Smith ("The Sound of Brands", Journal of Marketing, 2010) **[older]**: brand names with phonetic repetition were rated more favourably when spoken aloud than names without it or names only read; a one-letter change in an invented name could shift choice between identical ice creams. Excessive repetition, or sounds unlike natural language, backfired, and the effect was weaker when people already felt positive. — [ScienceDaily, 2010](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/10/101018092237.htm); [FoodNavigator-USA, 2010](https://foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2010/10/21/Repetitive-sounds-could-hold-secret-of-successful-brands); [phys.org, 2010](https://phys.org/news/2010-10-brand-music-ears.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a cute creature game, sound symbolism supports names with bilabials (p, b, m), front vowels, light fricatives, and diminutive endings (the game already uses -let and -ling: Pondlet, Dewling, Cinderling, Brookkin). Heavy voiced stops and long multi-word names push toward "big, strong, evolved, villain", which suits a battler more than a cosy shop.
- Pronounceability matters more than length: a two- or three-word real-word name is fine if every word is common; a short invented word fails if people can't spell it after hearing it.
- Alliteration or internal rhyme can make a cosy name stickier when heard (streams, word of mouth), but a doubled cutesy syllable risks "too much".
- Effect sizes in sound-symbolism studies are small and partly language-specific; use them to break ties between otherwise good candidates, not as a primary criterion.

### Gaps
- I couldn't open Klink (2000) or Yorkston & Menon (2004, "Frosh" vs "Frish" ice cream; a copy is on [Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/doc/220848833/yorkston)), so their effect sizes are unverified here.
- No study tests sound symbolism of whole game titles against Steam sales or wishlists; the creature-name evidence is from Pokémon names, not game titles.
- No source found on Hebrew-speaking or non-English audiences reading English game titles (relevant only if the game is localised).

## 4. How do cosy, cute or creature-collecting games name themselves, versus simulation and management games ("Simulator", "Tycoon", "Shop", "Story")? What does each pattern signal?

### Takeaway
Management words ("Manager", "Tycoon") correlate with the highest median Steam revenue of any common title words, but mainly because they label complex strategy games with dedicated buyers. "Simulator" now signals the streamer-friendly first-person job sim, a surging but very crowded subgenre where most entries earn little. Cosy games favour soft, place- or creature-based names, and cosy-coded words like "Farm" and "Little" have low medians, reflecting a crowded space.

### Cited Findings
- **Word data (GameDiscoverCo, Simon Carless, 5 Sept 2023):** median lifetime gross by title word, for words used in 100+ Steam titles: HD $63k (106 games, $63,082), Manager $33k, Remastered $32k, Tycoon $19k, Pro $16k, Gold $11k, Dead $5.2k, Black $4.5k; lowest: Jump $40, Maze $90, Ball $100, Tank $140. Farm $2k, Little $1k. Carless: "Strategy-centric names do well, likely due to game complexity"; the lowest words "smack of 'my first game' vibes"; "This doesn't mean that if you call your original IP game 'XX Gold Pro Remastered HD' you'll go big, tho!" — [GameDiscoverCo, 2023](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/whats-in-a-name-top-steam-game-titles); [Gigazine summary](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20230907-name-game-titles-steam); [PC Gamer coverage](https://www.pcgamer.com/what-do-the-names-of-bestselling-steam-games-have-in-common-words-like-manager-tycoon-remastered-and-hd). Gigazine reports that half the words have a median under $1k gross.
- **"Simulator" (GameDiscoverCo, 23 Sept 2025):** since Supermarket Simulator (Feb 2024; about $27m on Steam in 2024) there has been "a series of 'simulator' games that are easy to control"; the first-person sim is "one of the few surging new subgenres" on PC. But 38 games with "Simulator" in the title launched in Aug 2025 and only Waterpark Simulator had passed about $70k gross (next best: Returns Outlet Simulator about $69k, Parisian Brasserie Simulator about $62k). Success came with streamer reach (Waterpark's publisher belongs to YouTuber Caylus; CaseOh made a 19-episode series), and games where players "comically fail at jobs." — [GameDiscoverCo, 2025](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-rise-of-the-first-person-sim)
- **Cosy genre traits:** cosy games emphasise "non-violence and relaxation", are "meant to evoke feelings of warmth, comfort, and peace" (Wired), and centre on farming, gathering, growing and nurturing, with cute characters and low stakes. GamesIndustry.biz's Colin Campbell found only "a handful" of the "dozens" of games described as cosy on Steam came out before 2020, so the label is recent and crowded. Examples named: A Short Hike, Stardew Valley, Animal Crossing: New Horizons, The Spirit and the Mouse, Harvest Moon. — [Wikipedia: Cozy game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cozy_game)
- **Colon/subtitle use is highest in the Wholesome tag** (16% of titles vs 13% on average). — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Creature-named cosy games:** Gourdlets (renamed after its creatures, "sounds cute"). — [aunty games devlog](https://aunty-games.itch.io/gourdlets/devlog/385478/new-title-)
- Spry Fox's Cozy Grove reportedly began from a wish to make a "cozy" game inspired by Animal Crossing, and put the word in the title. — [Game Developer Q&A](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-designing-for-empathy-in-wholesome-life-sim-i-cozy-grove-i-); [The Avocado interview](https://the-avocado.org/2021/11/05/indie-developer-interview-spry-fox/) (from search snippet; not fully read)

### Inferences
- **"Simulator"** signals: first-person, realistic job, streamer comedy, often low price; it brings a ready audience but puts a game in a glut where most entries earn little, and may set expectations of a realistic job sim rather than a whimsical creature game.
- **"Tycoon"/"Manager"** signal spreadsheets, optimisation and growth curves; strong medians come from strategy buyers. Using them for a cosy creature game would promise a deeper management game than may be delivered.
- **"Shop"** (in my reading, e.g., shopkeeping games) signals the buy/sell loop but not what's sold; on its own it's generic.
- **"Story"** names (a Kairosoft-style or "Garden Story" pattern) signal a gentle, often pixel-art life sim; I found no data on them.
- Cosy and creature games tend to name **the world, the creatures, or a soft verb/place** rather than the mechanic. A name built on the creatures (Gourdlets pattern) fits a game whose star is a creature called the Pondlet.
- The word data is correlation, not causation, and is heavily shaped by genre: words reflect what kind of game it is, not a magic effect of the word.

### Gaps
- GameDiscoverCo's full word list (Google Doc) wasn't readable here, so medians for Simulator, Shop, Story, Life, Pet, Monster, Creature, Lab, Evolution, Idle and Clicker are unknown.
- No data found on how cosy creature-collecting games specifically (Slime Rancher, Ooblets, Cassette Beasts, Paleo Pines and similar) chose their names; I found no developer interviews explaining those names in this session.

## 5. Known pitfalls: generic dictionary words, names that sound like other genres, names that promise the wrong game, names that age badly

### Takeaway
The documented failures are: **bland/generic** (nothing to remember or search for), **misleading** (promises another genre or audience), **obscure** (words people don't know or can't spell), **controversial** (not safe to share), **forgettable** (even to the team), **too close to an existing name** (legal and search collisions), and **trend words** whose apparent success reflects genre, not the word.

### Cited Findings
- **Bland:** "Galaxy Rescue" suggested "nothing or a top-down arcade space shooter." — [Game Developer, 2016](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Wrong game:** "Word Challenge" for an alien shooter; Fitzsimons later judged "Slaves' Sunrise" better suited to a GTA-style game than his educational one. — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Not descriptive / too close to the studio's other game:** Dejobaan's "OOO!" title. — [Game Developer, 2011 [older]](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)
- **Phonetically close to another game, leading to a cease and desist:** Icarian to NyxQuest. — [same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)
- **Forgettable:** "Mystic Village" to "Gourdlets". — [aunty games devlog](https://aunty-games.itch.io/gourdlets/devlog/385478/new-title-)
- **Too unique to read:** "you don't want your title to be so unique that nobody knows what it is"; chains of generic words also blur together. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Cultural reading differs by region:** check meaning across audiences; focus groups from different cultures can mislead. — [Game Developer, 2016](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-the-wrong-name-can-kill-your-game)
- **Trademark/domain:** handles and domains being free don't prove the name is free. — [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/how-to-name-video-game-legal-licensing-indie-devs) (snippet)
- **"My first game" words:** title words like Jump, Maze, Ball, Tank have the lowest medians ($40 to $140) and "smack of 'my first game' vibes". — [GameDiscoverCo, 2023](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/whats-in-a-name-top-steam-game-titles)
- **Renaming is possible on Steam** until pre-release review, but costs momentum and assets; Bruce wanted a website, logo and screenshots ready before announcing. — [Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/editing/name); [Game Developer, 2011 [older]](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)

### Inferences
- A two-generic-word name ("Evolution" + "Shop") risks all three of bland, search collisions with other "Evolution" games, and the wrong genre (evolution and idle/merge games are a crowded mobile pattern in my experience, unverified here).
- Names that reuse a currently hot label ("Simulator", "Cozy") may age fastest if the label goes out of fashion, and they tie the game to a subgenre's expectations.
- Before committing: check Steam search, Google "<name> game", trademark databases, and how the name sounds when spoken next to well-known titles.

### Gaps
- I found no systematic evidence on names "ageing badly" for indie games, only anecdotes.
- I did not check here whether specific "Evolution"-named games or trademarks exist on Steam (out of scope for this note; another step should check).

## 6. Evidence on subtitles and colons ("Name: Descriptor") and tagline-style names

### Takeaway
About 13% of Steam titles use a colon, a share that isn't growing; it's highest in Wholesome (16%). Genre-keyword subtitles ("tinyBuild style") may help low-information buyers but can read as "cringe" to core PC audiences; Zukowski calls it worth experimenting with, not a trend. Steam lets an **official subtitle** appear on capsules, so a short evocative name plus an optional descriptive subtitle is allowed.

### Cited Findings
- **Colon frequency:** about 13% of Steam titles (2016 to 2025) use a colon; highest in Wholesome (16%) and Open World Survival Craft (16%), lowest in Puzzle Platformer (8.4%). The share using colons has dropped in recent years. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Keyword-stuffed subtitles** like "ALL WILL FALL: Physics-Based Survival City Builder" (a tinyBuild pattern) may suit low-information buyers who pick from the top of a list (as on mobile), but on Steam may look "cringe" to hardcore audiences; worth experimenting with, not a growing trend. — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Long titles aren't penalised:** title length doesn't correlate with sales; successful long titles include "Skyrim Special Edition" and "Age of Empires II: Definitive Edition". — [howtomarketagame.com, 2025](https://howtomarketagame.com/?p=4464)
- **Steam's capsule rules** allow only the game name and "any official subtitle" as text on store capsules. — [Steamworks: Graphical asset rules](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/assets/rules)
- **Older example of a tagline-style name:** Dejobaan's "1... 2... 3... KICK IT! (Drop That Beat Like an Ugly Baby)" replaced a non-descriptive name. — [Game Developer, 2011 [older]](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/in-depth-when-the-name-of-the-game-changes)

### Inferences
- For a cosy game, a colon subtitle is slightly more normal than average (Wholesome 16%), so "Short Name: soft descriptor" is acceptable to the audience, as long as the descriptor reads as part of the brand rather than keyword stuffing.
- A subtitle can carry the genre signal (creatures, shop, aquatic) so the main name can be more evocative and ownable, which addresses the descriptive-versus-evocative tension in section 2.

**Consolidated tests for judging candidates** (each traced above):
1. A good name is understood when shouted across a noisy room, and the listener can type it into Steam after hearing it once (Zukowski 2025).
2. A good name can be said correctly by a streamer on first read, and works both said and read (Zukowski 2025; Antichamber, 2011).
3. A good name, with the capsule art, lets a stranger guess the genre and something of what you do (Zukowski 2025, 2021).
4. A good name isn't bland or generic, and doesn't blend with other games' names (Fitzsimons 2016; Igor via secondary sources).
5. A good name doesn't promise a different genre, audience or tone (Fitzsimons 2016).
6. A good name uses words the audience knows, or a new word spelled as it sounds (Fitzsimons 2016; Zukowski 2025; Alter & Oppenheimer 2006).
7. A good name is memorable, even to its own team (Gourdlets).
8. A good name is safe to share for all ages, in every region it ships to (Fitzsimons 2016).
9. A good name isn't phonetically close to another game or trademark (NyxQuest, 2011; Wayline).
10. For a cute creature game, a good name's sounds lean soft and small (bilabials p/b/m, front vowels, diminutives) rather than heavy (voiced d/g, long names) (Kawahara and colleagues 2018 to 2023; Klink 2000).
11. A good name may repeat a sound for stickiness, in moderation (Argo, Popa & Smith 2010).
12. A subtitle, if used, is short, official, part of the brand, and adds genre signal rather than keyword stuffing (Zukowski 2025; Steamworks).

### Gaps
- No controlled data on whether a descriptive subtitle raises wishlists or click-through on Steam; Zukowski's evidence is frequency, not outcome.
- No data found on tagline-style names' performance.
